      How it got built

      GRADE 11 / 10 HR A WEEK / 40 WEEKS / TEAM OF SIX
      The full story

      We started by driving the kit car and writing down everything wrong with it. A wheel lifted off the ground when it accelerated, the turning circle was massive and the tyres kept slipping. Every problem we found became something we had to fix.

      I was Design Engineer and Driver. I designed the main body and a compact double wishbone suspension I took from F1 cars. The slices are fluorescent acrylic slotted into a HIPS base, so one LED strip underneath lights the whole car up for the dark section of the course. Some of the printed parts are recycled PETG.

      The tyres are TPU Air with an asymmetric rally tread. Turn the nozzle temperature up and it foams more, so the tyre comes out softer and grippier. They press onto PLA+ hubs with no glue, so we could swap them mid-testing.

      An Arduino runs the extras: headlights that switch on by themselves, a buzzer if the car tilts too far, and a servo tow bar you control from an Android app over Bluetooth. Lasers help line it up with the trailer.

      It isn't perfect. We never got the turning circle or the suspension as good as we wanted. GluCare and Zahra's Kitchen sponsored us, and whatever we didn't spend went to wildlife conservation and desert clean-ups.

      GRADES 11–12 / 12 HR A WEEK / 18 WEEKS A YEAR
      The full story

      Kessler syndrome is when debris from one collision causes the next one, and it keeps going until the orbit is useless. I modelled three shells, at 450, 550 and 850 km, to find each one's tipping point.

      Then I ran 200-year simulations to see how much collision avoidance you'd need to stay under it. At 550 km the answer is anywhere from 90.5% down to 0%. It depends on how much the orbits overlap.

      The question, the model and the paper are mine. Dr. Damián Pitalúa-García at DAMTP, Cambridge mentored me, and the paper is under review at the National High School Journal of Science.
